When *Squid Game* dropped on Netflix in September 2021, nobody—not even the streamer itself—predicted it would become the platform's most-watched series of all time. The Korean survival drama, directed by Hwang Dong-hyuk, didn't just break records; it rewrote the playbook for what non-English-language content could achieve globally. Here's how a darkly inventive show about desperate people playing deadly children's games became a cultural juggernaut.

## The Record That Changed Everything

Within weeks of its release, *Squid Game* became a phenomenon that transcended typical streaming buzz. **Netflix announced that the series had been watched by over 111 million accounts in its first 28 days**, demolishing the previous record held by *Bridgerton*. That number eventually climbed to **1.65 billion hours viewed** in the first four weeks alone, cementing its place as Netflix's biggest series launch ever.

The show's reach wasn't limited to Korea or even Asia. It topped Netflix's daily Top 10 in **94 countries**, including the United States, United Kingdom, and across Latin America and Europe. For context, most Korean dramas that gain international traction might crack the top 10 in a handful of markets. *Squid Game* dominated nearly every territory where Netflix operates.

## Why It Resonated Worldwide

*Squid Game*'s premise—456 debt-ridden contestants compete in a series of traditional Korean children's games with fatal consequences for the losers—struck a nerve far beyond its country of origin. The show's themes of economic inequality, desperation, and the dehumanizing nature of late-stage capitalism felt universal in 2021, as the world grappled with pandemic-era financial precarity.

Director Hwang Dong-hyuk reportedly conceived the idea in 2008 during the global financial crisis but struggled for over a decade to secure funding. By the time it arrived on Netflix, its social commentary felt even more timely. The visual contrast—bright, primary-colored sets hosting brutal violence, playground games turned lethal—created an instantly recognizable aesthetic that fueled viral moments on social media.

### The Accessibility Factor

Netflix's investment in subtitling and dubbing also played a crucial role. The series launched with **multiple dub tracks and subtitles in over 30 languages**, lowering the barrier for viewers who might typically skip subtitled content. While subtitle-versus-dub debates raged online, both options brought new audiences to Korean storytelling on an unprecedented scale.

The show's relatively self-contained episodes and high-concept premise made it easy to recommend and binge. You didn't need prior knowledge of Korean culture or K-drama conventions to follow the story—though eagle-eyed viewers discovered layers of cultural specificity that rewarded deeper engagement.

## Historic Emmy Recognition

The global success translated into awards recognition that made history. At the **2022 Primetime Emmy Awards**, *Squid Game* earned **14 nominations**—a record for a non-English-language series. More importantly, it converted several of those nominations into wins, breaking barriers that had stood for over seven decades.

**Lee Jung-jae won Outstanding Lead Actor in a Drama Series** for his role as Seong Gi-hun, becoming the first actor from a non-English-language series to win in that category. **Hwang Dong-hyuk won Outstanding Directing for a Drama Series**, another first for a non-English production. The show also picked up wins for stunt performance and guest actress (Lee You-mi).

![Emmy award statuette on stage with spotlight](/images/blog/why-squid-game-became-netflixs-biggest-show-ever-2/3.jpg)

These weren't token diversity nods—the competition included heavyweight English-language dramas like *Succession*, *Euphoria*, and *Ozark*. The Emmy wins validated what the viewership numbers had already proven: quality storytelling transcends language barriers, and Television Academy voters were finally catching up to what global audiences had been saying all along.

> 💡 Context: Prior to *Squid Game*, only a handful of non-English programs had won Emmys, mostly in limited categories like documentary or short-form series—never in major drama categories.

## The Ripple Effect on Korean Content

The show's success had immediate industry impact. Netflix announced a **significant increase in investment in Korean content**, eventually committing over $2.5 billion to Korean productions through 2023. Other streaming platforms accelerated their own Korean content acquisitions and production deals.

Korean dramas and films that might have remained regional hits suddenly found global audiences. The "Netflix effect" that *Squid Game* turbocharged brought attention to everything from period dramas to romantic comedies, horror films to variety shows. International viewers who discovered K-content through *Squid Game* often went down a rabbit hole of Korean entertainment.

### Industry Validation

For Korean creators, the Emmy wins represented long-overdue recognition. The Korean film and television industry had been producing world-class content for decades—*Oldboy*, *The Handmaiden*, *Reply 1988*, *My Mister*—but often struggled for visibility beyond dedicated cinephile and drama fan communities. *Squid Game* brought that quality to the forefront of global pop culture conversation.

## What Made It Different

Several factors converged to create *Squid Game*'s perfect storm of success:

- **High-concept premise**: Easy to explain, immediately intriguing
- **Universal themes**: Economic anxiety and survival transcended cultural boundaries  
- **Visual distinctiveness**: The show's aesthetic became instantly iconic and meme-able
- **Binge-worthy structure**: Nine episodes with escalating stakes and cliffhangers
- **Timing**: Released when Netflix's global reach had matured and audiences were primed for non-English content
- **Algorithmic amplification**: Netflix's recommendation system pushed it aggressively based on early positive signals

The show also benefited from not having to carry the weight of existing IP or franchise expectations. It was a fresh, original story that audiences discovered organically (or so it felt, despite Netflix's promotional muscle).

## The Lasting Impact

*Squid Game*'s records still stand as a high-water mark for non-English content on global streaming platforms. While the show's second season has been confirmed (and highly anticipated), its legacy is already secure: it proved that Korean stories, told in Korean, could become the biggest entertainment phenomenon in the world.

The series changed industry assumptions about what "mainstream" content looks like and who it's for. It opened doors for creators working outside English-language markets and demonstrated to risk-averse executives that subtitles aren't the audience barrier they long believed them to be.

For international viewers, *Squid Game* was often a gateway into Korean entertainment—a first encounter with K-drama storytelling, Korean social commentary, and the depth of talent in Korea's creative industries. The fact that it achieved this not by imitating Western formulas but by being unapologetically Korean in its cultural specificity made the breakthrough all the more significant.
